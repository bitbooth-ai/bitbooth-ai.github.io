Part the First: Flora

On flowers that grow from piping bags rather than soil

Paeonia cremora

The Blushing Peony

The flagship of the genus. Petals are piped one by one upon a rotating flower nail, then lifted onto the cake with scissors and a held breath. Blooms year-round, unlike its garden cousin, and never wilts — it is eaten long before it gets the chance.

Kingdom
Confectionery
Order

Habitat

Flowering

Field note: guests will ask if it's real. It is real. It's just also butter.

Dahlia sacchara

The Ivory Dahlia

Remarkable for its geometric precision: concentric rings of petals in warm ivory, each placed at an angle the mathematics of which the baker refuses to explain, calling it merely "practice." A patient species.

Rosa lilacina

The Impossible Lilac

Occurs in a shade of lilac unknown to natural rose populations, achievable only in a piping bag at the exact right pressure on a good day. Frequently sighted in bouquet-box formations alongside Paeonia cremora (see Plate I) — the full florist's window, compressed into one sliceable square.

Order

Field note: the bouquet cakes (pl. VI) carry whole colonies of these.

Part the Second: Exotic Species

Specimens of the Siam lineage — ลูกชุบ — a royal Thai craft, made at a kitchen table

Fructus siamensis

The Glass Fruit · Look Choup

A master of mimicry. Sculpted from sweetened mung bean, hand-painted, then dipped in glaze until it shines like glass — this species imitates mangoes, chillies, and cherries so convincingly that dinner guests have been recorded arguing about it. Related species: Rosa crustula (rose pia), a flaky pastry rose that shatters politely upon first bite.

Kingdom
Confectionery
Order
Siamensia
Origin
royal Thai kitchens, generations deep
Defence mechanism
being mistaken for décor
Gloss
glass-like; do not water

Field note: the chilli one fools somebody at every single party.

Part the Third: Fauna

On the Macaronidae — shell-dwelling creatures with hand-piped faces and strong opinions

Each face is piped by hand, and therefore each specimen exhibits its own personality — a phenomenon the baker describes as "mildly chaotic, entirely on purpose." The bear (Ursus macaron) is the most frequently requested; the lemon (Citrus ridens, "the laughing lemon") the most frequently impersonated. The December migration arrives every Christmas and sells out with alarming speed.
